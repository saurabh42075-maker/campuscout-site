Getting paid to study in the US: graduate assistantships

An assistantship is a job at the university that comes attached to your admission. A good one covers tuition and pays you a stipend. Here is how they work, who realistically gets them, and the rules you will live under.

Last checked 30 September 2026

The basics

Three kinds of assistantship

Names vary between universities, but almost every assistantship is one of these.

01

Teaching assistantship (TA)

You help teach undergraduate courses — running tutorials or labs, grading, holding office hours. Funded by the department.

02

Research assistantship (RA)

You work on a professor's research project, usually paid from that professor's grant. Often the work becomes part of your own thesis.

03

Graduate assistantship (GA)

Administrative or support work elsewhere in the university — an office, a library, student services. Less common for international students at admission.

What a package usually includes

Read the offer letter line by line

A typical full assistantship includes the items below. None of them is guaranteed — each university and each offer is different, so check what yours actually says.

  • A tuition waiver — full or partial
  • A monthly stipend, paid as a salary for the work
  • Help with health insurance — often a subsidy, sometimes full cover
  • An expected workload, commonly 10–20 hours a week

We do not quote stipend figures here: they vary too much by university, field and city to be useful as a single number. Compare offers against the local cost of living, not against each other.

Being straight with you

Who realistically gets one

More likely

  • PhD applicants — many US PhD programmes fund every student they admit
  • Research-heavy fields, especially science, engineering and technology
  • Applicants with research experience, a strong statement of purpose and good references
  • Applicants who have identified professors whose work matches theirs

Less likely

  • Master's applicants — many taught master's programmes offer little or no funding at admission
  • Fields with few research grants
  • Applications that read as generic rather than written for that department
  • Applicants who apply after the funding deadline, which is often earlier than the admission deadline

Some master's students find a TA or on-campus job after their first semester rather than at admission. That can help, but it should not be the plan you borrow against.

The timeline

Working backwards from an autumn start

Exact dates are set by each department. This is the usual shape.

1

12–15 months before

Shortlist programmes and professors. Check each department's funding page — look for a clear statement on whether admitted students are funded.

2

December – January

Applications for funded consideration. Many departments only consider applicants who meet an early deadline for funding.

3

February – April

Offers arrive. Under the Council of Graduate Schools April 15 Resolution, students are not obliged to accept an offer of financial support before 15 April at participating universities.

The rules

What your F-1 student status allows

While you study

  • On-campus work, including an assistantship, is limited to 20 hours a week while school is in session
  • Full-time on-campus work is allowed during official breaks
  • The university's funding letter is part of the proof of funds for your I-20, the form you need for the visa

Changes from 15 September 2026

  • A DHS rule published on 17 July 2026 replaces open-ended “duration of status” with a fixed admission period — up to the length of your programme, and no more than four years
  • Staying longer, for example for a longer PhD, means applying to USCIS for an extension
  • The grace period after your programme or OPT ends is cut from 60 days to 30

After the degree

Optional Practical Training (OPT)

Graduates can work in the US on OPT for 12 months. Graduates in approved STEM fields can apply for a further 24-month STEM OPT extension. Both survived the July 2026 rule, but DHS has said it is reviewing OPT, so treat future availability as a risk to plan around rather than a certainty.

Questions families ask

Straight answers

Is an assistantship the same as a scholarship?

No. A scholarship or fellowship is money with no work attached. An assistantship is employment — you are paid for teaching or research work, and the tuition waiver usually comes with the job.

Can I get one for a master's degree?

Sometimes, but far less often than for a PhD. If funding is essential for your family, look hard at PhD programmes and at master's programmes that state clearly that they fund students.

Do I need to contact professors before applying?

For research assistantships it often helps, because RA funding comes from a professor's grant. Keep it short and specific to their work. Some departments ask applicants not to — check the department's guidance first.

Does an assistantship cover everything?

Rarely. Fees that are not tuition, the visa, flights, a deposit on housing and the first month before the stipend arrives usually fall on you. Budget for them separately.
